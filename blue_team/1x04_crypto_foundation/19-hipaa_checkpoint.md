## 1. Scope, legal baseline and interpretation

**Exercise assumption:** This task expressly stipulates that MedDefense is a **HIPAA covered entity**. Apply the US HIPAA Security Rule to its electronic protected health information (ePHI) for this exercise; do not mistake that assumption for independently verified real-world jurisdiction. Other potentially applicable privacy regimes (including GDPR) require separate evaluation.

**Current law versus proposals:** This audit uses the operative 45 CFR Part 164, Subpart C, especially §§164.306, 164.308, 164.312 and 164.316, **as reviewed on 6 November 2026**. The HHS cybersecurity update proposed on 6 January 2025 (RIN 0945-AA22) remained in the 2026 Unified Agenda's **Long-Term Actions** with a projected final action in 2027; **a proposal must not be applied as if already effective**. [H1–H7]

**Required vs Addressable is not required vs optional:** The **standards** at §164.312(a)(1), (d) and (e)(1) are mandatory. The **encryption/decryption implementation specification** at §164.312(a)(2)(iv), and the **transmission-encryption specification** at §164.312(e)(2)(ii), are **Addressable**. Under §164.306(d)(3), MedDefense must assess each against actual ePHI risks, implement it if reasonable and appropriate, or document why it is not appropriate and deploy a reasonable equivalent alternative if one exists. Not deploying encryption without a documented and supportable decision is **not** compliant handling; where the standard can otherwise be met, reasons for omitting either control must likewise be documented. HIPAA does **not** prescribe AES-256 specifically. [H1–H4]

**Decision vocabulary:** `No / significant gap` = affirmative evidence of a security weakness plus no supplied documentation of a lawful, effective response; `Not demonstrated` = the evidence is insufficient to conclude compliance or noncompliance; `Partially evidenced` = some safeguards are documented but important ePHI paths remain exposed. These are **audit-readiness determinations**, not final HHS violation findings. A proposed plan, a passing Fedora LUKS lab, or a third-party TLS scan is **not** proof of production compliance.

**Important distinctions:** Encryption-at-rest protects media in an offline state, **not** plaintext returned by an authorized SQL session. A signed DICOM transfer is not automatically encrypted. `SSL enabled` is not `SSL required`, LDAP **signing** is not LDAP **confidentiality**, and a VPN tunnel does not automatically protect traffic after it reaches the destination network. None of the cited findings alone proves an actual breach.

## 5. Could MedDefense pass a HIPAA security audit today? (One-paragraph answer)

**On the documented scenario evidence, MedDefense should not expect to pass a rigorous HIPAA Security Rule audit today:** identifiable patient records in `ehr-db-01` and images in PACS are stored without evidenced at-rest encryption; PostgreSQL TLS is optional, DICOM containing patient identifiers travels in cleartext, and NAS backup copies are unencrypted, while corresponding written §164.306(d)(3) risk/alternative determinations and operational tests have not been supplied. The **most critical at-rest encryption deficiency** is the lack of an assessed and justified protection mechanism for the **EHR patient database and its backup copies** under §164.312(a)(2)(iv), compounded by the **required transmission-security** failure exposure for cleartext DICOM and possibly plaintext ePHI-bearing database sessions under §164.312(e)(1)/(e)(2)(ii). An auditor would also challenge identity/account attribution and the lack of approved risk treatment evidence; however, **these are defensible audit concerns, not a formal determination of a HIPAA violation** without inspecting actual controls, risk decisions, contracts, configurations and records. An addressable encryption provision is **not an absolute mandate to use AES-256 on every ePHI system**, but a documented, reasonable and effective response to these high-risk paths is necessary. [H1–H6]
