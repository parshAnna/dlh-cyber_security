## Assessment basis

**R** refers to the registry and its source keys: O (onboarding), I (incidents), D (billing diagnostics), W (walk-through), C (controls), M (MRI scenario) and S (scan). No live testing was performed. Ratings express the consequence of a credible compromise, not its likelihood; weak controls, old operating systems and current incidents inform context but do not by themselves determine asset criticality.

The assignment scale is applied as follows: **Critical** for direct patient-safety threats, a material breach of healthcare confidentiality obligations, or halted clinical operations; **High** for significant disruption, financial loss or sensitive-data exposure; **Medium** for moderate disruption recoverable through standard procedures; **Low** for minimal impact. Confidentiality ratings distinguish direct clinical-record services from assets with uncertain or narrower data scope. They are impact judgments, not legal determinations that a specific incident requires notification or incurs a particular penalty. No patient count, breach cost or recovery target is invented.

For each category, **Overall Criticality = maximum(Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability)**, ordered Low < Medium < High < Critical. A category rating reflects its most consequential credible service-level compromise; it does not mean that every component inherits that rating. The coverage table assigns each registry ID to one group, while acknowledging cross-group dependencies. Missing critical applications such as the pharmacy system retain provisional ratings based on their documented function; unknown ownership does not imply low importance.

## Top 5 Most Critical Assets

Ranking breaks Critical-rating ties by breadth of clinical dependency, immediacy of potential patient harm and ability to recover or substitute the service. A clinical fleet is explicitly treated as one operational asset group in rank 4; it is not a claim that a particular pump is more important than another without knowing patient assignments. This is an asset-impact ranking, not an incident-containment sequence or a fixed disaster-recovery runbook.

### 1. ehr-db-01 — EHR database (A-037; logical store A-112)

This database holds the clinical information served by ehr-srv-01. Loss or corruption can undermine record access and treatment decisions across the EHR workflow; the documented nine-hour EHR outage forced physicians onto paper. It ranks first because of the breadth of its clinical-record dependency and the combined confidentiality, integrity and availability consequences. The database is backed up, but no full recovery exercise establishes rapid restoration; restoring an application alone cannot replace missing or untrustworthy records. The rank does not imply the application server or network can be omitted from EHR recovery.

### 2. Pharmacy management system (A-093)

This application supplies medication dosage information across all three sites. Incident C demonstrates the failure mode: an update script overwrote dosage values for six hours, and detection depended on a pharmacist comparing a printed reference. It ranks second because inaccurate information could influence medication decisions at multiple sites even while the application appears available. Hosting, ownership and recovery arrangements remain unknown, so these must be identified urgently; no actual patient injury is claimed from the incident.

### 3. Central Cisco core switch (A-087)

The draft topology places the core between Central's departmental connectivity and its servers, with intersite access terminating through the FortiGate. A core failure or damaging configuration change could prevent multiple clinical services from reaching their users simultaneously, giving it broader operational reach than an individual department server. It ranks below the direct clinical-information services because harm is mediated by connectivity and depends on the real topology, but above department-specific imaging because its dependency scope is wider. Only one core is documented; redundancy and failover are unknown, so this ranking is provisional pending topology validation, not proof of a confirmed single point of failure.

### 4. BD Alaris infusion-pump fleet (A-061–A-067 plus the registry's aggregate pump entries)

These devices deliver treatment and receive dosage updates over the network. Integrity or functional availability compromise can directly threaten patients receiving infusions, making their clinical importance independent of whether a specific published vulnerability is exploitable. The operational fleet ranks fourth because the preceding assets have documented hospital-wide or three-site dependencies; for a patient actively receiving a critical infusion, the attached pump can take immediate precedence over every other ranked asset. Approximately 120 pumps are reported in onboarding, whereas the scan summary accounts for approximately 117; neither a network outage stopping all infusions nor a simultaneous fleet compromise is assumed.

### 5. pacs-srv-01 — PACS imaging server (A-038)

PACS supports diagnostic image availability and is the required destination for MRI study transfer. The MRI service alone processes approximately 45 studies per day, so unavailable or incorrect imaging can delay or misdirect diagnosis and treatment. PACS ranks above the individual MRI controller for this list because it serves the shared imaging function rather than one modality, while its explicit backup exclusion makes recovery especially uncertain. It ranks below the broader information/network dependencies and direct infusion-treatment devices; this does not make time-sensitive imaging optional. The MRI controller WS-RAD-01 remains Critical within G2 despite not appearing separately in the top five.

## Validation priorities and practical limits

Confirm clinical downtime tolerances and safe workarounds with department leads; identify the pharmacy host and its approved data flows; validate core/VMware/AD failover and EHR dependencies; confirm bedside offline behavior with biomedical staff; and establish recovery evidence for PACS and EHR. A tested alternate terminal, failover path or safe manual process can lower a component's outage consequence without changing the importance of the clinical service itself.

NAS-01 remains a Critical recovery dependency even though it is outside the top five: it may become a leading operational priority during ransomware recovery. Similarly, billing-srv-01's documented mining activity warrants immediate incident handling even though its intrinsic business criticality is High. These distinctions prevent a static asset ranking from being mistaken for the order in which every incident must be handled.
