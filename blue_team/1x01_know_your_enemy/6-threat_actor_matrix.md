# MedDefense Health Systems — Threat Actor Matrix

## Assessment Basis

This matrix consolidates the [T0 intelligence summary](0-threat_landscape_summary.md), [T1 actor taxonomy](1-threat_actor_taxonomy.md), [T2 ransomware assessment](2-ransomware_assessment.md), [T3 insider analysis](3-insider_assessment.md), [T4 social engineering analysis](4-social_engineering_analysis.md) and [T5 vendor assessment](5-supply_chain_assessment.md). Exposure references use the First Watch [Gap Analysis](../1x00_first_watch/12-gap_analysis.md), [Asset Registry](../1x00_first_watch/7-asset_registry.md) and [Criticality Assessment](../1x00_first_watch/8-criticality_assessment.md). T8 technical-vector analysis is not yet available; technical entry paths below derive from T0–T2 and the internal evidence.

**Likelihood** is a qualitative assessment for the next 12 months: **High** means a strong match between sector behavior and MedDefense's exposure, **Medium** means a credible threat with less evidence of occurrence, and **Low** means limited evidence of targeted interest. For negligent insiders, it means harmful mistakes or unsafe practices, not deliberate targeting. **Capability** separates resources from sophistication; legitimate privileges can make a low-resource insider dangerous. Dossier statistics are attributed historical evidence, not MedDefense-specific probabilities.

The First Watch Top 5 are: **(1)** EHR database `ehr-db-01` (A-037; logical data store A-112), **(2)** pharmacy system (A-093), **(3)** Central core switch (A-087), **(4)** infusion-pump fleet (A-061–A-067 plus aggregate devices), and **(5)** PACS imaging server (A-038). All are Critical assets. A public website or billing server can be an actor's initial target even when the most serious subsequent impact concerns a Top 5 asset.

## Top 3 Priority Ranking

### 1. Ransomware Groups — High likelihood, Critical potential impact

Ransomware combines MedDefense's strongest external target-profile match with the potential to interrupt multiple clinical services and expose patient information simultaneously. January's four-day billing interruption establishes local history; T2's nearby attacks reinforce relevance without proving a common operator. T0 F1 reports theft before encryption in 73% of its healthcare ransomware cases, explaining why patient-data access and isolated backups both matter. GAP-015/GAP-016/GAP-017/GAP-007 connect entry, movement, theft and recovery destruction, while the PACS backup exclusion under GAP-003 makes imaging recovery particularly vulnerable. This combination places ransomware first; prioritize verified public-service remediation, restricted internal access, timely detection and tested protected recovery.

### 2. Insider (Negligent) — High likelihood, potentially Critical clinical consequences

Unsafe internal practices are directly documented, so their likelihood rests on more than a general sector statistic: technicians share PACS sessions, patient copies reach an unmanaged NAS, and an administrator stores powerful credentials in plaintext. The access involved can expose EHR records, undermine PACS accountability or enable a later external attacker; unsafe operational changes can also affect medication information or service continuity. GAP-011/GAP-014/GAP-020/GAP-022 explain why ordinary workload pressures can have disproportionate consequences. Negligence ranks above opportunistic attacks because it repeatedly occurs inside existing access boundaries and can involve clinical or administrative privileges from the outset. Prioritize individual access, practical data-handling workflows and reviewed automation; intent is benign, but impact need not be.

### 3. Unskilled/Opportunistic Attacker — High exposure likelihood, High observed operational impact with Critical escalation potential

Automated attackers can encounter MedDefense without selecting hospitals deliberately, and the billing miner demonstrates an unresolved compromise pattern that repeated restarts did not establish as removed. Its observed effect is resource consumption and billing disruption, not confirmed EHR theft or clinical injury. However, GAP-015/GAP-012/GAP-016 offer plausible paths from weak or unmanaged entry points toward more important systems, making the potential impact greater than the initial symptom. This category ranks third because present exposure and local compromise evidence outweigh the lower assessed frequency of deliberate insider theft, state espionage or ideological targeting. Prioritize resolving GAP-008's root cause and verifying exposed-service patches; a purchased foothold could later be used by ransomware affiliates, so actor categories can overlap.

Malicious insiders remain a serious Medium-likelihood threat: misuse by a privileged employee could cause Critical harm and would take immediate priority if detected. They fall outside this strategic Top 3 because the supplied evidence supports more recurring negligence and opportunistic exposure, not because intentional disclosure is acceptable. Nation-state capability alone does not offset MedDefense's weak strategic-target fit, and hacktivist publicity objectives currently have less documented clinical reach. Reassess the ranking after new incidents, verified threat intelligence or changes in research partnerships and vendor access; it is not a substitute for incident-response triage.
